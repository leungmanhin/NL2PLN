1. Entity classification and type membership: English classifies individuals as members of kinds, categories, and roles. Examples: A robin is a bird. Alice is a doctor. Mars is a planet.

2. Named entities and proper reference: Proper names, titles, and unique labels refer to specific people, places, organizations, or artifacts. Examples: Ada visited Paris. The Nile crosses Egypt. NASA launched the probe.

3. Common nouns and sortal constraints: Nouns introduce kinds of entities and constrain what can sensibly fill an argument role. Examples: The cat slept. A student solved the problem. The river overflowed.

4. Properties and attributes: Adjectives, nouns, and predicative phrases ascribe qualities, colors, sizes, materials, and other attributes. Examples: The ball is red. The table is wooden. The child seems tired.

5. Gradable properties and degree: Many adjectives and adverbs express values on scales and can be modified by degree words. Examples: The tower is very tall. The soup is slightly salty. The road is too narrow.

6. Non-gradable and categorical properties: Some predicates express binary or conventional classifications rather than scalar degrees. Examples: The switch is off. The animal is female. The document is confidential.

7. Relational nouns and inherent relations: Some nouns require or imply another participant, such as kinship, ownership, authorship, or part relations. Examples: Maria is Leo's sister. Plato was Socrates' student. The car's engine failed.

8. Possession and ownership: English expresses possession, control, association, and belonging with possessives and have constructions. Examples: Sam owns a bicycle. The girl's book is missing. The company has three offices.

9. Identity, equality, and distinctness: English states that two descriptions refer to the same entity or to different entities. Examples: Clark Kent is Superman. The winner was Elena. Alice is not Bob.

10. Existence and absence: English asserts or denies that entities or events exist, occur, or are present. Examples: There is a spider in the sink. A solution exists. No milk remains.

11. Definiteness and indefiniteness: Articles and determiners signal familiarity, uniqueness, identifiability, or introduction of a new discourse referent. Examples: A dog barked. The dog ran away. This book is heavy.

12. Demonstratives and deixis: Words like this, that, here, and there depend on speaker context, location, or salience. Examples: This cup is mine. Put the box there. That woman waved.

13. Mass nouns, count nouns, and substance reference: English distinguishes countable individuals from masses and substances, often affecting quantification and measurement. Examples: Three apples fell. Some water spilled. The sand was warm.

14. Measures, units, and quantities: Noun phrases can specify amount, weight, distance, price, volume, or other measured values. Examples: The rope is five meters long. She bought two liters of milk. The package weighs ten kilograms.

15. Plurality and number: Singular and plural marking distinguishes one entity from multiple entities and interacts with agreement and quantification. Examples: The bird sings. The birds sing. Two chairs broke.

16. Collective and distributive readings: Plural subjects can act as a group or as individuals. Examples: The students gathered in the hall. The students each received a badge. The boys carried a piano together.

17. Part-whole and meronymy: English expresses components, portions, members, and wholes. Examples: The wheel is part of the car. The handle broke off the cup. Three players left the team.

18. Set membership and inclusion: Sentences can describe membership in sets, subsets, classes, and groups. Examples: Erin belongs to the choir. Every square is a rectangle. The blue marbles are in the bag.

19. Generic statements and kind reference: English can make claims about kinds, typical members, or general truths rather than particular individuals. Examples: Tigers have stripes. A dog needs exercise. The whale is a mammal.

20. Actions and events with thematic roles: Verbs describe events involving roles such as agent, patient, theme, experiencer, source, goal, and recipient. Examples: Lina opened the door. The boy gave Mia a flower. The wind broke the window.

21. Agent and patient relations: Many clauses distinguish the doer of an action from the entity affected by it. Examples: The chef chopped the onions. The dog chased the cat. Nora painted the fence.

22. Themes, recipients, sources, and goals: Transfer and motion verbs identify what moves, who receives it, where it comes from, and where it goes. Examples: Omar sent Dana a file. The bird flew from the tree to the roof. She poured water into the glass.

23. Instruments and means: English can specify tools or methods used to perform an action. Examples: Ben cut the bread with a knife. She unlocked the door using a card. They traveled by train.

24. Comitative and accompaniment relations: Sentences can state that participants act together or accompany one another. Examples: Maya walked with Leo. The teacher met with the parents. I ate lunch with my sister.

25. Manner and adverbial modification: Adverbs and adverbial phrases describe how an action is performed. Examples: The dancer moved gracefully. He answered in a whisper. The machine stopped suddenly.

26. Result states and change of state: English describes transitions from one state to another and the resulting condition. Examples: The ice melted. She painted the wall blue. The door became locked.

27. Stative predicates and states: Some expressions describe conditions, locations, possession, emotions, or relations rather than events. Examples: Kim knows French. The vase is on the shelf. The child loves music.

28. Voice and argument alternations: Active, passive, middle, and related constructions change how participants are syntactically profiled. Examples: The guard opened the gate. The gate was opened by the guard. This book reads easily.

29. Causation: English expresses that one event, state, or participant brings about another event or state. Examples: The storm caused flooding. Ravi made the baby laugh. The alarm woke everyone.

30. Enablement and prevention: Sentences can state that something permits, blocks, or makes an event possible or impossible. Examples: The key allowed her to enter. The fence kept the dog outside. The rain prevented the match.

31. Purpose and intended result: Infinitives, purpose clauses, and prepositional phrases express goals or reasons for actions. Examples: She studied to pass the exam. He saved money for a trip. They whispered so the baby would sleep.

32. Spatial relations and location: Prepositions and locative phrases describe containment, contact, proximity, orientation, and relative position. Examples: The keys are in the drawer. The lamp is above the desk. The school is near the park.

33. Motion, path, and direction: English encodes movement through space, including source, path, goal, and trajectory. Examples: The cat ran across the street. She walked into the room. The ball rolled down the hill.

34. Distance and spatial measurement: Sentences can compare or measure separation, proximity, and extent. Examples: The station is two miles away. The chair is closer to the door than the sofa. The river stretches for fifty kilometers.

35. Temporal location and calendrical reference: English locates events at times, dates, clock times, and periods. Examples: The meeting starts at noon. We arrived on Monday. The festival happened in July.

36. Temporal duration and intervals: English expresses how long states or events last and the interval over which they hold. Examples: She slept for eight hours. The museum was closed during winter. He waited from noon until three.

37. Temporal ordering and sequencing: Clauses and adverbs express before, after, simultaneity, and event order. Examples: Wash your hands before dinner. She called after the show ended. I read while the soup cooked.

38. Tense: Verb forms and auxiliaries locate situations relative to speech time or another reference time. Examples: Nina walks to school. Nina walked to school. Nina will walk to school.

39. Aspect: English distinguishes ongoing, completed, repeated, prospective, and perfect situations. Examples: I am reading the report. I have read the report. I used to read every night.

40. Frequency and habitual aspect: Adverbs and aspectual constructions express how often events occur or whether they are customary. Examples: She often jogs. The train arrives every hour. We usually eat dinner at seven.

41. Iteration and repetition: English can describe events occurring multiple times or again. Examples: He knocked three times. The baby laughed again. The light flashed repeatedly.

42. Existential quantification: Determiners and pronouns assert that at least one entity satisfying a description exists. Examples: Someone knocked. A bird is singing. There is a problem.

43. Universal quantification: English can state that a claim holds for all members of a set. Examples: Every student passed. All roads were closed. Each ticket has a number.

44. Cardinality and exact number: Numerals and numerical determiners specify exact or bounded counts. Examples: Three birds landed. Exactly two keys are missing. At least five people called.

45. Proportional and fractional quantification: Expressions describe proportions, fractions, percentages, or parts of a group. Examples: Most guests arrived early. Half of the cookies are gone. Twenty percent of the voters abstained.

46. Indefinite and vague quantification: English uses imprecise quantity expressions such as many, few, several, enough, and some. Examples: Many trees fell. Few people noticed. She has enough money.

47. Negative quantification: Determiners and pronouns can combine quantification with negation. Examples: No students failed. Nobody answered. Nothing moved.

48. Exceptives and inclusion modifiers: English can include or exclude subsets using expressions such as except, only, also, and besides. Examples: Everyone except Tom left. Only Maria solved the puzzle. Alex also came.

49. Negation: English denies propositions, predicates, events, quantities, or constituents with not, never, no, and related forms. Examples: The door is not open. She never smokes. No train arrived.

50. Lexical negation and antonymy: Some words encode negative or opposite meanings without explicit sentential negation. Examples: The answer is incorrect. The plan failed. The room was empty.

51. Modality of ability: Can, be able to, and related expressions describe capacities or skills. Examples: Lina can swim. The machine is able to detect smoke. I cannot lift the box.

52. Modality of possibility and probability: English expresses that something may, might, could, or is likely to be true. Examples: It may rain. The package might arrive today. She is likely to win.

53. Modality of necessity and obligation: Must, have to, need to, and should express requirements, necessities, or duties. Examples: You must stop. The plants need water. Employees have to wear badges.

54. Modality of permission and prohibition: English states what is allowed, forbidden, or authorized. Examples: You may leave now. Visitors can take photos. Students must not enter the lab.

55. Epistemic versus deontic modality: The same modal forms can express evidence-based inference or rules and obligations. Examples: The lights are on, so Sam must be home. You must submit the form. She may be in her office.

56. Conditionals and if-then relations: Conditional clauses state dependencies between antecedents and consequents. Examples: If it rains, the picnic will move indoors. You pass if you score eighty. If the switch is off, the light stays dark.

57. Counterfactual conditionals: English can describe hypothetical situations contrary to fact or unlikely possibilities. Examples: If I had a car, I would drive. If she had called, we would have waited. If the vase fell, it would break.

58. Comparison of greater, lesser, and equal degree: Comparative constructions order entities or events on scales. Examples: Ana is taller than Ben. This route is less dangerous than that one. The two boxes are equally heavy.

59. Superlatives and extremal comparison: Superlatives identify maximal or minimal members relative to a comparison class. Examples: Kai is the fastest runner. This is the least expensive option. Everest is the highest mountain.

60. Similarity and difference: English compares entities by likeness, identity of properties, or contrast. Examples: This copy is like the original. The twins look different. Her plan is the same as mine.

61. Conjunction: English combines entities, predicates, clauses, and propositions with and and similar coordinators. Examples: Kim and Lee arrived. The soup is hot and spicy. She opened the door and entered.

62. Disjunction: English expresses alternatives, inclusive choices, or exclusive choices with or and either-or. Examples: Tea or coffee is available. Either call me or send an email. The key is in the drawer or on the table.

63. Coordination of unlike constituents: English can coordinate phrases of different semantic types when they share a larger function. Examples: She is smart and a good leader. Pat worked quickly and with care. I need your name and where you live.

64. Scope interactions: Quantifiers, negation, modals, and other operators can take different semantic scopes. Examples: Every student read a book. A guard did not check every bag. You may not enter.

65. Propositional attitudes: Verbs like believe, know, think, hope, fear, and doubt relate agents to embedded propositions. Examples: Maya believes that the door is locked. Jon knows that the train left. I doubt that he is awake.

66. Desire, intention, and preference: English expresses wants, plans, goals, and preferences toward actions or states. Examples: Rosa wants to leave. The team intends to win. I prefer coffee to tea.

67. Knowledge and factivity: Some predicates presuppose the truth of their complements, while others do not. Examples: Eva knows that the safe is open. Leo realized that he was late. Sam thinks that the safe is open.

68. Communication and speech reports: English reports saying, asking, promising, warning, and other communicative acts. Examples: Mia said that the gate was open. The officer warned us about ice. He promised to return.

69. Questions and interrogatives: English asks for truth values, entities, quantities, times, places, reasons, or choices. Examples: Did the train arrive? Who opened the window? Which road leads north?

70. Imperatives and directives: English issues commands, requests, instructions, or permissions through imperative forms. Examples: Close the door. Please sit down. Take one tablet daily.

71. Reasons and explanations: English expresses why something happens or why someone acts. Examples: The game stopped because it rained. She left since she was tired. The road closed due to flooding.

72. Focus-sensitive expressions: Words such as only, even, also, and too depend on focused alternatives. Examples: Only Dana laughed. Even the teacher was surprised. Sam also bought a ticket.

73. Anaphora and pronoun reference: Pronouns and other anaphoric expressions refer back to entities, events, or propositions in discourse. Examples: Maria dropped the glass, and it shattered. Leo saw Omar, and he waved. I missed the train, which annoyed me.

74. Reflexives and binding: Reflexive pronouns require suitable antecedents, often within a local clause. Examples: Alice praised herself. The boys blamed themselves. Ken introduced himself to the class.

75. Discourse reference and accessibility: Later expressions can depend on entities introduced by earlier indefinite or definite phrases. Examples: A dog entered. The dog barked. If a farmer owns a donkey, he feeds it.

76. Relative clauses: Relative clauses restrict or add information about nouns. Examples: The woman who called is my aunt. I found the book that you lost. The car, which was red, sped away.

77. Restrictive versus appositive modification: Some modifiers narrow reference, while others add parenthetical information. Examples: Students who registered may enter. My brother, who lives in Rome, is visiting. The vase on the shelf is cracked.

78. Complement clauses: Clauses can function as arguments of verbs, adjectives, or nouns. Examples: She claimed that the test was easy. It is clear that he lied. The idea that time bends is strange.

79. Control constructions: An unspoken subject of an infinitive is understood from another argument. Examples: Sara promised to help. Sara persuaded Tom to help. The children tried to sleep.

80. Raising constructions: Some predicates appear to assign a subject syntactically while the subject belongs semantically to an embedded predicate. Examples: Tom seems to be tired. The plan is likely to fail. Kim appears to know the answer.

81. Ellipsis and omitted material: English often leaves understood material unspoken, requiring recovery from context. Examples: Alice ordered tea, and Bob coffee. I can swim, and she can too. Who came? Maria.

82. Ambiguous attachment: Prepositional phrases and modifiers can attach to different constituents, changing meaning. Examples: I saw the man with a telescope. She ate the cake on the plate. The spy watched the guard near the door.

83. Preposition polysemy: Prepositions have multiple related meanings across space, time, cause, possession, and topic. Examples: The book is on the table. The show starts on Monday. She wrote a book on birds.

84. Idioms and conventionalized meanings: Some expressions have meanings not predictable from their parts. Examples: He kicked the bucket. She spilled the beans. They broke the ice.

85. Metaphor and metonymy: English often uses one domain or associated entity to refer to another. Examples: The White House issued a statement. Time is running out. The ham sandwich wants the check.

86. Presupposition triggers: Certain words and constructions imply background assumptions. Examples: Dana stopped smoking. The king of France is bald. It was Maya who broke the cup.

87. Implicative and implicature-like predicates: Some verbs imply success, failure, or nonoccurrence of an embedded event. Examples: She managed to escape. He failed to arrive. They avoided hitting the deer.

88. Adversative and concessive relations: English expresses contrast between expected and actual outcomes. Examples: Although it rained, we played. She was tired but happy. Even though he studied, he failed.

89. Alternative and exhaustive meanings: Some constructions require selecting among alternatives or excluding unmentioned options. Examples: Which color did you choose? I chose red rather than blue. The answer is either yes or no.

90. Probability, uncertainty, and evidential strength: English marks confidence, uncertainty, rumor, appearance, or evidence. Examples: Apparently, the train is late. She probably forgot. It seems that the roof is leaking.

91. Evaluation and sentiment: English expresses judgments, attitudes, goodness, badness, desirability, or importance. Examples: The movie was excellent. It is unfortunate that she left. This task is important.

92. Norms, rules, and social roles: English states what is required, permitted, forbidden, customary, or appropriate in a social system. Examples: Drivers should stop at red lights. Guests may not smoke here. Judges must be impartial.

93. Numerical approximation and vagueness: English can express inexact numerical values and vague thresholds. Examples: About twenty people came. The trip took nearly an hour. She is over six feet tall.

94. Ordinals and ranking: Ordinal expressions identify position in an ordered sequence. Examples: Lee finished first. The third drawer is empty. This is her second attempt.

95. Event nominalization: Nouns can denote events, processes, or results derived from verbs. Examples: The destruction of the bridge shocked us. Her arrival was late. The investigation continued.

96. Subsective, intersective, and intensional adjectives: Modifiers differ in whether they preserve category membership or introduce intensional readings. Examples: A red car is a car. A skilled surgeon is a surgeon. A former senator is not a senator.

97. Quotation, mention, and language use: English can use words to refer to expressions themselves rather than their usual denotations. Examples: Cat has three letters. She said hello. The word bank is ambiguous.

98. Polarity items and licensing: Some expressions occur naturally only under negation, questions, conditionals, or other licensing environments. Examples: I did not see anyone. Did you ever visit Rome? If you need any help, call me.
