1. Entity classification and kind membership: English classifies individuals as instances of types, classes, or kinds. Examples: Fido is a dog. The tulip is a flower. A robin is a bird.

2. Properties and attributes: English assigns qualities, states, colors, sizes, ages, and other attributes to entities. Examples: The apple is red. The tower is tall. Maria is tired.

3. Relational attributes: Some attributes relate one entity to another, such as age, height, price, owner, or role. Examples: The book is Maria's. The table is two meters long. Sam is the captain of the team.

4. Identity and equivalence: English can state that two descriptions refer to the same entity or that two values are equal. Examples: Clark Kent is Superman. The winner is Aisha. My home is this apartment.

5. Existence and nonexistence: English asserts that something exists, appears, is present, or is absent. Examples: There is a cat on the roof. No milk remains in the fridge. A solution exists.

6. Definite and indefinite reference: English distinguishes familiar, uniquely identifiable, and newly introduced entities. Examples: A dog barked. The dog ran away. I found a key.

7. Proper names and named entities: English uses names for specific people, places, organizations, times, and artifacts. Examples: Ada visited Paris. Google hired Lina. Everest is in Asia.

8. Pronouns and coreference: Pronouns and other referring expressions can point back to previously mentioned entities. Examples: Nora dropped the glass because she slipped. The dog chased the cat, but it escaped. Omar saw himself in the mirror.

9. Deixis and context-dependent reference: Words such as I, you, here, now, this, and that depend on the speech context. Examples: I am here now. Put this on that shelf. You should leave tomorrow.

10. Count nouns, mass nouns, and substances: English distinguishes countable objects from uncountable substances and abstract masses. Examples: I bought three chairs. She drank water. There is sand in the shoe.

11. Plurality and number: English expresses singularity, plurality, and groups of multiple entities. Examples: The child is sleeping. The children are sleeping. Three birds landed.

12. Collective entities and group reference: Groups can be treated as wholes or as collections of members. Examples: The committee met at noon. The team wore blue jerseys. The crowd dispersed.

13. Part-whole relations: English expresses components, portions, membership, and containment within larger wholes. Examples: The wheel is part of the car. The handle broke off the cup. Alice is a member of the club.

14. Possession and ownership: English marks ownership, custody, association, and control. Examples: Ben owns a bicycle. Sara has a laptop. The dog's collar is red.

15. Actions and events with thematic roles: English describes events involving roles such as agent, patient, theme, recipient, source, goal, and instrument. Examples: Lena cut the bread with a knife. Tom gave Maya a book. The wind broke the window.

16. Agent and patient relations: English identifies who performs an action and who or what is affected. Examples: The chef chopped the onions. The cat scratched the sofa. The storm damaged the roof.

17. Transfer and ditransitive events: English describes giving, sending, showing, teaching, and other transfer events involving a giver, item, and recipient. Examples: Mira gave Jonah a ticket. The teacher taught the class algebra. Paul sent a letter to Kim.

18. Instrument and means: English specifies tools, mechanisms, or means used to perform actions. Examples: She opened the door with a key. He traveled by train. They fixed the leak using tape.

19. Benefactive and malefactive roles: English marks who benefits from or is harmed by an event. Examples: Ana baked Leo a cake. The delay cost us money. He held the door for the visitor.

20. Experiencers and psychological states: English expresses perception, emotion, cognition, and bodily experience with experiencer roles. Examples: Priya fears spiders. The music pleased the audience. I felt a sharp pain.

21. Perception events: English describes seeing, hearing, smelling, tasting, and feeling, often with perceivers and perceived objects. Examples: I saw a hawk. Emma heard the alarm. The dog smelled smoke.

22. Speech and communication events: English describes saying, asking, telling, promising, warning, and other communicative acts. Examples: Lina told Omar the news. The sign warns drivers. He promised to return.

23. Motion events: English expresses movement, movers, paths, sources, goals, and manners of motion. Examples: The child ran into the house. A bird flew over the lake. The ball rolled down the hill.

24. Spatial location: English places entities in regions, positions, and configurations. Examples: The cup is on the table. The keys are under the mat. The school is near the park.

25. Spatial containment and support: English distinguishes being in, inside, on, atop, attached to, or supported by something. Examples: The coin is in the box. The vase is on the shelf. The poster is on the wall.

26. Spatial orientation and direction: English expresses left, right, front, back, above, below, north, and other orientations. Examples: The bank is north of the station. The lamp is to the left of the sofa. The plane flew upward.

27. Distance and proximity: English describes nearness, farness, adjacency, and separation. Examples: The hotel is close to the beach. The village is far from the city. The two chairs are next to each other.

28. Paths and boundaries: English describes movement through, across, along, past, into, out of, and around regions. Examples: We walked through the forest. The truck crossed the bridge. The mouse ran out of the hole.

29. Temporal tense: English locates situations in past, present, and future time. Examples: Maya cooked dinner. Maya is cooking dinner. Maya will cook dinner.

30. Temporal ordering: English expresses before, after, during, while, since, until, and simultaneous events. Examples: I called after lunch. She slept while the rain fell. We waited until sunset.

31. Dates, times, and calendar expressions: English refers to specific times, recurring calendar points, and relative dates. Examples: The meeting starts at 3 p.m. He arrived on Monday. We leave next week.

32. Duration and intervals: English expresses how long states or events last. Examples: She studied for two hours. The festival lasted three days. I have lived here since 2020.

33. Aspect and event structure: English distinguishes ongoing, completed, repeated, beginning, continuing, and ending situations. Examples: The baby is sleeping. They have finished the work. The engine stopped running.

34. Habitual and generic aspect: English describes regular behaviors, customs, and characteristic truths. Examples: Sara walks to work. Tigers hunt at night. My grandfather reads after dinner.

35. Frequency: English states how often events occur. Examples: I rarely eat meat. The bus arrives every ten minutes. They often visit their cousins.

36. Existential quantification: English states that at least one entity satisfying a description is involved. Examples: Someone knocked. A bird is singing. There is a problem.

37. Universal quantification: English states that a claim holds for all members of a class or set. Examples: Every student passed. All dogs need water. Each ticket has a number.

38. Cardinality and exact number: English expresses exact, minimum, maximum, and approximate quantities. Examples: Three students left. At least five people called. About twenty birds gathered.

39. Generalized quantifiers: English uses many, most, few, several, enough, half, and similar expressions. Examples: Most guests arrived early. Few children disliked the game. Several cars were parked outside.

40. Proportions and percentages: English expresses fractions, ratios, and shares of sets or quantities. Examples: Half the class voted. Ten percent of the apples are rotten. Two thirds of the water evaporated.

41. Distributive, collective, and cumulative readings: Plural expressions can apply to individuals separately, groups together, or quantities cumulatively. Examples: The students each carried a box. The students lifted a piano. Three boys ate five pizzas.

42. Scope interactions: Quantifiers, negation, modality, and other operators can interact in ways that affect interpretation. Examples: Every student read a book. A guard is outside every door. Kim did not answer every question.

43. Negation: English denies propositions, events, properties, or existence. Examples: The door is not open. Sam did not call. No one entered.

44. Constituent negation and contrastive negation: English can negate a specific word or phrase rather than the whole clause. Examples: I want tea, not coffee. She spoke to Tom, not to Anna. The shirt is not red but orange.

45. Negative polarity items: Certain expressions appear naturally in negative, interrogative, conditional, or downward-entailing environments. Examples: I did not see anyone. Do you have any money? If you ever need help, call me.

46. Modality of ability: English expresses whether an entity has the capacity or skill to do something. Examples: Nora can swim. The machine is able to print labels. I cannot lift that box.

47. Modality of possibility: English expresses that something may or might be true or occur. Examples: It may rain tonight. The keys might be in the car. This road could be closed.

48. Modality of necessity: English expresses that something must be true, required, or unavoidable. Examples: You must wear a helmet. The answer has to be correct. Water needs to be clean.

49. Permission and prohibition: English expresses what is allowed or forbidden. Examples: You may leave early. Visitors can park here. Students must not enter the lab.

50. Obligation and advice: English expresses duties, norms, recommendations, and weaker requirements. Examples: You should apologize. Employees are supposed to sign in. Drivers ought to slow down.

51. Causation: English states that one event, action, or condition brings about another. Examples: The rain caused flooding. Sam made the baby laugh. The alarm woke everyone.

52. Purpose and intention: English expresses goals, intended outcomes, and reasons for actions. Examples: Mina saved money to buy a car. He whispered so the baby would not wake. They built a fence for privacy.

53. Result and consequence: English describes outcomes produced by events or states. Examples: The glass fell and broke. She studied hard, so she passed. The room became quiet.

54. Comparison of degree: English compares entities along scales such as size, speed, age, cost, and quality. Examples: Lee is taller than Max. This road is less dangerous than that one. The blue bag is as heavy as the red bag.

55. Equality and similarity: English states that entities share equal values or resemble each other. Examples: The two ropes are the same length. This copy is identical to the original. Ava looks like her sister.

56. Superlatives and extremes: English identifies maximal or minimal members with respect to a property. Examples: Dana is the fastest runner. This is the least expensive option. Everest is the highest mountain.

57. Ordinal relations and ranking: English expresses first, second, last, next, previous, and ranked order. Examples: Maya finished second. The last train left. The next chapter is shorter.

58. Conjunction: English combines entities, predicates, events, clauses, and conditions with and and related forms. Examples: Alice and Ben arrived. The soup is hot and spicy. Kim washed the dishes and dried them.

59. Disjunction and alternatives: English expresses alternatives, choices, and inclusive or exclusive possibilities. Examples: Tea or coffee is available. You can call or email me. Either the key is lost or the door is locked.

60. Coordination of unlike categories: English can coordinate noun phrases, verb phrases, adjectives, clauses, and mixed structures. Examples: The box is heavy and hard to move. She sang and played guitar. I need a pen and to find my notes.

61. Conditionals: English expresses dependencies between conditions and consequences. Examples: If it rains, the picnic will move indoors. You will pass if you study. If the light is red, stop.

62. Counterfactual conditionals: English describes hypothetical situations contrary to fact or presented as remote possibilities. Examples: If I had a car, I would drive there. If she had left earlier, she would have arrived on time. If this were gold, it would be heavier.

63. Unless and exceptive conditions: English expresses negative or exceptional conditions. Examples: I will go unless it rains. No one may enter except staff. The store is open every day except Sunday.

64. Propositional attitudes: English reports mental relations to propositions, including belief, knowledge, doubt, hope, fear, and expectation. Examples: Lena believes the train is late. Omar knows that the door is locked. I doubt that he is home.

65. Desire, intention, and preference: English expresses wants, wishes, plans, goals, and preferences. Examples: Nina wants to leave. They intend to build a house. I prefer tea to coffee.

66. Factivity and presupposed truth: Some expressions suggest that their complements are already true or taken for granted. Examples: Mia knows that the file is missing. I regret that I shouted. Sam realized that the gate was open.

67. Intensional and opaque contexts: In belief, desire, search, and modal contexts, descriptions may not behave like ordinary references. Examples: Leo is looking for a unicorn. Maya wants a new assistant. Kim believes the masked singer is famous.

68. Manner adverbs: English modifies events by describing how they are performed. Examples: She opened the box carefully. The dog barked loudly. He answered politely.

69. Degree adverbs and intensifiers: English modifies the strength or extent of properties, events, and quantifiers. Examples: The soup is very hot. She almost won. The box is completely full.

70. Temporal adverbs: English uses adverbs to locate or relate events in time. Examples: He arrived yesterday. She will call soon. They already finished.

71. Aspectual adverbs: English marks continuation, completion, repetition, and change with adverbs such as still, already, again, and anymore. Examples: The child is still awake. I already ate. She knocked again.

72. Relative clauses: English uses clauses to restrict or add information about nouns. Examples: The man who called is my uncle. The book that I borrowed is missing. I visited Paris, which I love.

73. Restrictive and nonrestrictive modification: Modifiers can identify which entity is meant or add parenthetical information. Examples: Students who study pass. My brother, who lives in Rome, called. The red car is mine.

74. Apposition: English places noun phrases side by side to identify or describe the same entity. Examples: My friend Sara arrived. Paris, the capital of France, is crowded. The winner, a young pianist, smiled.

75. Complement clauses: English embeds full propositions as subjects, objects, or complements. Examples: That she left surprised me. I think that he is honest. The problem is that we are late.

76. Control constructions: English allows an unspoken participant in an embedded action to be understood from another phrase. Examples: Anna promised to help. Ben persuaded Cara to leave. The children tried to sleep.

77. Raising constructions: English can make an entity appear as the subject of a higher verb while semantically belonging to an embedded clause. Examples: Sam seems to be tired. The plan is likely to fail. Nina appears to know the answer.

78. Passive voice and voice alternations: English changes perspective on event participants, often foregrounding the affected entity. Examples: The window was broken by the storm. The cake was eaten. Maria received a letter.

79. Causative-inchoative alternation: English distinguishes causing a change from undergoing a change. Examples: Kim broke the vase. The vase broke. The sun melted the ice.

80. Reflexives and reciprocals: English expresses actions directed back to the same participant or mutually among participants. Examples: Leo blamed himself. The twins helped each other. The countries traded with one another.

81. Ellipsis and omitted material: English often leaves recoverable words or phrases unspoken. Examples: Kim ordered tea, and Lee coffee. I can play guitar, and Maya can too. Sam likes chess more than Nora does.

82. Anaphoric one, do so, and substitution: English uses substitute expressions to avoid repetition. Examples: I need a pen, preferably a blue one. Lina washed the car, and Omar did so too. This cake is better than the other one.

83. Questions and interrogatives: English asks for truth values, entities, times, places, reasons, manners, and quantities. Examples: Did Ana call? Who opened the door? How many tickets remain?

84. Embedded questions: English can include questions inside larger declarative or interrogative sentences. Examples: I know where she lives. Tell me who won. Do you remember when it started?

85. Imperatives and directives: English issues commands, requests, invitations, and instructions. Examples: Close the window. Please sit down. Let us begin.

86. Requests and indirect speech acts: English often expresses intended actions indirectly through questions or statements. Examples: Can you pass the salt? I would like you to leave. It is cold in here.

87. Speech reports and quotation: English reports utterances directly or indirectly. Examples: Ana said, I am ready. Ana said that she was ready. The notice says the road is closed.

88. Tense sequence and reported time: English shifts or preserves tense in embedded and reported contexts. Examples: Leo said he was tired. Mia says she is leaving tomorrow. I knew that the train had departed.

89. Generics and kind-level statements: English makes broad claims about kinds rather than specific individuals. Examples: Dogs bark. The tiger is striped. A smartphone uses electricity.

90. Exceptions and default generalizations: English states rules with exceptions or tendencies rather than absolute universals. Examples: Birds usually fly. All students except Nina passed. Normally, water boils at this temperature.

91. Vagueness and gradability: Many English predicates have context-sensitive boundaries and degrees. Examples: The room is warm. The child is tall. The pile is small.

92. Approximation and imprecision: English expresses rough quantities, times, and descriptions. Examples: About fifty people came. The trip took roughly an hour. She is around six feet tall.

93. Measurement and units: English expresses quantities using units, dimensions, and measurement scales. Examples: The board is two meters long. The jar holds one liter. The package weighs five kilograms.

94. Rates and speeds: English expresses quantities per unit of time, distance, or other measure. Examples: The car traveled sixty miles per hour. The printer makes ten pages per minute. The rent is two thousand dollars a month.

95. Monetary and value expressions: English states prices, costs, values, wages, and payments. Examples: The ticket costs ten dollars. She paid thirty euros. The house is worth a million dollars.

96. Age expressions: English expresses age and age comparisons. Examples: The baby is six months old. Omar is older than Lina. This tree is centuries old.

97. Change of state: English describes entities becoming, remaining, or ceasing to be in states. Examples: The leaves turned yellow. The soup became cold. The door stayed open.

98. State versus event distinctions: English distinguishes static conditions from dynamic occurrences. Examples: The vase is on the table. The vase fell. Maya knows French.

99. Achievements, accomplishments, activities, and states: English verbs differ in temporal shape and completion. Examples: She reached the summit. He built a house. They ran in the park.

100. Incremental themes and affected objects: Some events progress as their objects are consumed, created, or transformed. Examples: Kim ate the sandwich. Nora wrote a letter. The workers painted the wall.

101. Polarity, contrast, and focus: English highlights alternatives or contrasts using stress, clefts, and focus-sensitive words. Examples: It was Maya who called. Only Sam passed. Even Leo smiled.

102. Additive and scalar particles: English uses words such as also, too, even, only, and just to relate alternatives and expectations. Examples: Nina also came. I only ate one cookie. Even the teacher was surprised.

103. Presupposition triggers: Definite descriptions, again, stop, continue, too, and similar expressions carry background assumptions. Examples: The king of the island arrived. Lee stopped smoking. Maya came too.

104. Implicature and pragmatic enrichment: English often conveys more than it explicitly states through context and conversational expectations. Examples: Some guests left early. The soup is warm. Can you reach the shelf?

105. Lexical ambiguity and polysemy: English words can have multiple related or unrelated meanings. Examples: The bank closed at five. The boat reached the bank. She opened a new account.

106. Structural ambiguity: A sentence can have multiple meanings because phrases can attach or group differently. Examples: I saw the man with a telescope. Old men and women waited. She discussed the plan on Monday.

107. Modifier attachment: English modifiers may apply to different nearby expressions. Examples: She photographed the bird in the park. I bought a book with pictures. The student from Spain with glasses spoke.

108. Idioms and fixed expressions: English includes multiword expressions whose meanings are not fully predictable from their parts. Examples: She kicked the bucket. He spilled the beans. They broke the ice.

109. Light verb constructions: English uses semantically light verbs with nouns to express events. Examples: She took a walk. He made a decision. They had a discussion.

110. Phrasal verbs: English verb-particle combinations can have compositional or idiomatic meanings. Examples: She looked up the word. The plane took off. He gave up.

111. Nominalization: English turns events, properties, or relations into nouns. Examples: The destruction of the city shocked us. Her arrival was late. The measurement took an hour.

112. Comparative subdeletion and differential comparison: English compares degrees with explicit or implicit differences. Examples: The table is two inches wider than the door. The lake is deeper than the river is wide. She is much faster than me.

113. Correlatives and proportional comparison: English expresses paired changes across two scales. Examples: The more you practice, the better you get. The colder it gets, the more fuel we use. The faster he runs, the sooner he arrives.

114. Similarity, difference, and distinction: English states that entities match or differ in properties or identity. Examples: These two keys are different. The twins look alike. This answer differs from yours.

115. Set membership and inclusion: English expresses membership in groups and containment among categories. Examples: Dana belongs to the choir. Whales are mammals. This folder includes three files.

116. Selection and choice: English marks choosing one or more alternatives from a set. Examples: Pick a card. She chose the blue dress. Select any two options.

117. Exclusion and inclusion: English states what is included, excluded, added, or removed. Examples: The price includes breakfast. Taxes are not included. Remove the broken pieces.

118. Ordering and sequencing of events: English describes series of actions in chronological or procedural order. Examples: First wash the rice. Then add water. Finally cover the pot.

119. Purpose clauses and rationale clauses: English distinguishes intended goals from explanatory reasons. Examples: She left early to avoid traffic. He stayed home because he was sick. I called so that you would know.

120. Reason and explanation: English gives causes, justifications, and evidence for claims. Examples: The road is wet because it rained. Since you are here, we can start. He must be home, for the lights are on.

121. Concession and contrast: English expresses unexpected coexistence or opposition between facts. Examples: Although it rained, we played outside. The bag is small but heavy. Even though she was tired, she continued.

122. Temporal recurrence and schedules: English describes events occurring on repeated days, cycles, or schedules. Examples: The train leaves every morning. The shop closes on Sundays. We meet twice a month.

123. Ability versus opportunity: English distinguishes capacity from circumstantial chance. Examples: Lina can speak French. Lina can speak now because the room is quiet. The door can open from inside.

124. Permission versus possibility ambiguity: English modal verbs can express either allowedness or factual possibility. Examples: You can leave after lunch. The answer can be wrong. Students may use calculators.

125. Epistemic modality and evidential flavor: English expresses degrees of certainty and evidence. Examples: She must be at work. He might have forgotten. Apparently, the train is delayed.

126. Attitude toward events and evaluation: English expresses approval, surprise, importance, likelihood, and desirability. Examples: It is good that you came. It is surprising that she won. It is likely that we will wait.

127. Predicative versus attributive modification: English adjectives can appear as predicates or inside noun phrases, sometimes with different meanings. Examples: The stars are visible. The visible stars are bright. The responsible person apologized.

128. Intersective and nonintersective adjectives: Some adjectives simply add a property, while others depend on the noun or change classification. Examples: The red car stopped. Olga is a former senator. He is a skillful surgeon.

133. Quantification over events: English can quantify not only over entities but over occurrences. Examples: Every time she visits, she brings flowers. Some meetings last too long. No accident happened today.

134. Bare plurals and bare mass terms: English uses nouns without determiners for generic, existential, or kind-level meanings. Examples: Cats like warmth. I bought rice. Students were waiting outside.
