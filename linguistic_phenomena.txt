1. Entity classification and type membership: English states that an individual belongs to a category or kind. Examples: Fido is a dog. Every oak is a tree. The red object is a cube.

2. Named entities and proper names: English uses names to refer to particular people, places, organizations, or objects. Examples: Alice lives in Paris. Google acquired the startup. Mount Everest is tall.

3. Properties and attributes: English assigns qualities, colors, sizes, shapes, and other attributes to entities. Examples: The apple is red. The box is heavy. Mira has blue eyes.

4. Attribute values and measurements: English expresses measurable properties with units, dimensions, and values. Examples: The table is two meters long. The water weighs five kilograms. The room is 20 degrees Celsius.

5. Relations between entities: English describes binary or multi-place relations such as kinship, ownership, membership, and adjacency. Examples: Emma is Liam's sister. The key belongs to Nora. The lamp is next to the bed.

6. Possession and ownership: English expresses having, owning, containing, and associated possession. Examples: Carla owns a bicycle. The house has three bedrooms. Ben's phone is on the desk.

7. Part-whole relations: English describes components, portions, members, and structural parts of larger entities. Examples: The wheel is part of the car. A slice of cake is on the plate. The team has eleven players.

8. Identity, equality, and difference: English states that two descriptions refer to the same entity or to distinct entities. Examples: Clark Kent is Superman. The morning star is the evening star. Tom is not Jerry.

9. Existence and absence: English asserts that something exists, occurs, or is missing. Examples: There is a cat in the garden. A solution exists. No chairs remain.

10. Stative predicates and states: English describes conditions that hold over time rather than punctual actions. Examples: The door is open. Maya knows French. The soup contains carrots.

11. Agent-patient events: English describes actions with participants such as the doer and the affected entity. Examples: Alice broke the vase. The dog chased the cat. Sam opened the window.

12. Thematic roles beyond agent and patient: English marks roles such as recipient, beneficiary, source, goal, and instrument. Examples: Nina gave Omar a book. Lee cut the bread with a knife. The package came from Boston.

13. Experiencer and stimulus events: English describes perception, emotion, and cognition with an experiencer and something perceived or felt. Examples: Dana heard a bell. Ravi fears spiders. The movie amused the children.

14. Change of state and result: English expresses transitions from one state to another and resulting conditions. Examples: The ice melted. The room became quiet. Kim painted the wall blue.

15. Creation and destruction events: English describes bringing things into existence or eliminating them. Examples: The chef baked a cake. The fire destroyed the barn. The company built a bridge.

16. Transfer and exchange: English expresses movement of possession, information, or goods between participants. Examples: Sara sent Tom a message. The cashier gave Mia a receipt. They traded cards.

17. Motion and path: English describes movement with source, path, direction, and destination. Examples: The bird flew into the tree. Leo walked from school to home. The ball rolled across the floor.

18. Spatial containment and support: English expresses being in, on, inside, under, or supported by something. Examples: The coins are in the jar. The book is on the shelf. The cat is under the table.

19. Spatial orientation and distance: English locates entities relative to other entities using direction, proximity, and alignment. Examples: The bank is north of the park. The chair is near the window. The picture hangs above the sofa.

20. Deictic spatial relations: English uses context-dependent spatial terms tied to a speaker or viewpoint. Examples: Put the box here. The exit is over there. Bring the chair closer.

21. Temporal location: English locates events at times, dates, and intervals. Examples: The meeting starts at noon. Tara arrived on Monday. The store opens in June.

22. Temporal duration: English expresses how long states or events last. Examples: The concert lasted two hours. Ben slept for nine hours. The battery worked all day.

23. Temporal ordering and simultaneity: English expresses before, after, during, while, and simultaneous events. Examples: Pat left before Kim arrived. The lights failed during the storm. Nora cooked while Eli cleaned.

24. Tense and aspect: English marks past, present, future, ongoing, completed, and perfective viewpoints. Examples: Ava is reading. Ava has read the report. Ava will read the report tomorrow.

25. Iteration and repeated events: English indicates that an event occurs multiple times. Examples: The bell rang three times. Liam knocked again. The dog kept barking.

26. Frequency and habitual aspect: English describes how often something happens or what is customary. Examples: Maya jogs every morning. The train usually arrives late. Owls hunt at night.

27. Existential quantification: English states that at least one entity satisfying a description exists. Examples: A student is waiting. Someone called. There is a problem.

28. Universal quantification: English states that a claim holds for every member of a class. Examples: Every bird has wings. All tickets are valid. Each child received a sticker.

29. Negative quantification: English expresses that no entities of a relevant kind satisfy a condition. Examples: No student failed. Nobody answered. None of the boxes are empty.

30. Cardinality and numerical constraints: English specifies exact, minimum, maximum, or comparative numbers of entities. Examples: Three cats slept. At least two guards remained. Fewer than five people came.

31. Proportional and vague quantification: English uses terms such as most, many, few, several, and half. Examples: Most guests arrived early. Few birds stayed. Half of the apples are ripe.

32. Quantifier scope interactions: English can contain multiple quantified expressions whose relative interpretation matters. Examples: Every student read a book. A guard checked every bag. Two reviewers approved each paper.

33. Definiteness and indefiniteness: English distinguishes familiar, unique, specific, and nonspecific references. Examples: A dog barked. The dog barked again. I need a doctor.

34. Anaphora and coreference: English uses pronouns and repeated descriptions to refer back to earlier entities. Examples: Alice found a wallet and she returned it. The boy dropped his glass. Maria saw Anna, and Anna waved.

35. Demonstratives and indexicals: English uses context-dependent expressions such as this, that, I, you, now, today, and yesterday. Examples: This cup is empty. I am hungry now. Yesterday was cold.

36. Plurality, mass nouns, and count nouns: English distinguishes individuals, groups, substances, and countable units. Examples: Three chairs are broken. The rice is warm. Several bottles of water are on the table.

37. Collective and distributive readings: English can describe a group acting together or members acting separately. Examples: The students gathered in the hall. The students each wrote an essay. The committee met at noon.

38. Generic and kind-level statements: English expresses generalizations about kinds rather than particular individuals. Examples: Tigers have stripes. A whale is a mammal. Dogs are loyal animals.

39. Sentential and predicate negation: English denies propositions, events, or states. Examples: The door is not open. Sam did not call. The machine does not work.

40. Constituent and lexical negation: English negates specific phrases or uses negative words and prefixes. Examples: Not every guest arrived. The answer is incorrect. Lena chose the nonviolent option.

41. Modality of ability: English expresses what someone or something can do. Examples: Priya can swim. The robot is able to lift boxes. This key can open the gate.

42. Modality of possibility and probability: English expresses what may, might, or could be the case. Examples: It may rain tonight. The train might be delayed. The noise could be a motor.

43. Modality of necessity and obligation: English expresses what must, has to, or should occur. Examples: Drivers must stop here. You have to submit the form. The medicine should be refrigerated.

44. Modality of permission and prohibition: English expresses what is allowed or forbidden. Examples: Students may use calculators. You can park here. Visitors must not feed the animals.

45. Causation: English states that one event or state brings about another. Examples: The storm caused flooding. The joke made Nina laugh. Heat melted the wax.

46. Purpose and intention: English expresses goals, reasons, and intended outcomes. Examples: Leo studied to pass the exam. Mia opened the window so that the room would cool. They saved money for a trip.

47. Means and method: English describes how an outcome is achieved. Examples: Omar fixed the leak by tightening the valve. The chef sliced the bread with a knife. They entered through the back door.

48. Comparison of degree: English compares entities by greater, lesser, or equal amounts of a property. Examples: Ana is taller than Ben. This road is less busy than that road. The two boxes are equally heavy.

49. Superlatives and ranking: English identifies the maximum, minimum, best, first, or otherwise ranked entity in a set. Examples: Zoe is the fastest runner. This is the oldest building in town. Kai chose the cheapest ticket.

50. Ordinals and sequence: English describes position in an ordered series. Examples: The second chapter is short. Maya finished third. The last train leaves at midnight.

51. Conjunction: English combines entities, events, properties, or propositions with and and related forms. Examples: Alice and Bob arrived. The cup is clean and dry. Nora cooked dinner and washed the dishes.

52. Disjunction: English expresses alternatives using or and related constructions. Examples: Tea or coffee is available. Call me or send a message. The key is in the drawer or on the shelf.

53. Exclusive and inclusive alternatives: English can suggest that alternatives are mutually exclusive or allow more than one. Examples: You may have soup or salad. The password contains a digit or a symbol. Either Kim or Lee will present.

54. Conditionals: English expresses dependence between a condition and a consequence. Examples: If it rains, the picnic will move indoors. If the alarm rings, leave the building. You get a discount if you pay today.

55. Counterfactual conditionals: English describes hypothetical situations contrary to fact or uncertain alternatives. Examples: If I had left earlier, I would have caught the train. If the vase were stronger, it would not break. Were Sam here, he would help.

56. Unless, only if, and necessary conditions: English marks special conditional relations. Examples: The game continues unless it rains. You can enter only if you have a ticket. Unless Dana calls, we will wait.

57. Propositional attitudes of belief and knowledge: English embeds propositions under mental-state verbs. Examples: Nora believes that the door is locked. Eli knows that the meeting is tomorrow. Kim suspects that the file is missing.

58. Desires, intentions, and plans: English expresses wants, hopes, goals, and intended actions. Examples: Maya wants to leave. Ravi hopes that the team wins. Lina plans to buy a car.

59. Communication and reported content: English reports what people say, ask, promise, or tell. Examples: Sam said that the road was closed. The guide told us that the museum opens at ten. Nina asked whether the package arrived.

60. Factivity and presupposed truth: Some English verbs and constructions imply that embedded content is taken as true. Examples: Leo realized that the door was open. Mia regretted that she missed the bus. Omar forgot that the meeting was today.

61. Evidentiality and source of information: English indicates how information was obtained or how reliable it seems. Examples: Apparently, the train is late. I heard that the shop closed. The sky looks stormy.

62. Manner adverbs: English modifies events by describing the way they occur. Examples: The dancer moved gracefully. Ben answered quickly. The child whispered softly.

63. Degree adverbs and intensifiers: English modifies the strength or extent of properties and events. Examples: The soup is very hot. The task is almost finished. The room is completely dark.

64. Temporal adverbs and aspectual adverbs: English uses adverbs to position or shape events in time. Examples: The guests arrived early. The printer is still running. Maya already ate.

65. Restrictive relative clauses: English narrows the reference of a noun phrase by adding a modifying clause. Examples: The book that Ana borrowed is missing. The person who called left a message. I saw the dog that chased the cat.

66. Nonrestrictive clauses and appositives: English adds extra information without restricting reference. Examples: My brother, who lives in Rome, is visiting. Paris, the capital of France, is crowded. Luna, a rescue dog, sleeps by the door.

67. Complement clauses: English embeds clauses as subjects, objects, or complements of verbs and adjectives. Examples: That the door is open worries me. I think that Ben is right. It is clear that the road is closed.

68. Passive voice and argument alternations: English can foreground the affected entity and background or omit the agent. Examples: The window was broken by Sam. The cake was eaten. The door opened.

69. Control and raising constructions: English links an unspoken subject in an embedded clause to another participant. Examples: Dana tried to leave. Omar persuaded Lina to stay. Maya seems to understand.

70. Questions and interrogatives: English asks for entities, truth values, quantities, times, reasons, and manners. Examples: Who opened the door? Did the train arrive? How many tickets remain?

71. Imperatives and directives: English issues commands, requests, instructions, and permissions. Examples: Close the window. Please bring the file. Do not touch the wire.

72. Ellipsis and fragments: English often omits recoverable material from context. Examples: Alice ordered tea, and Bob coffee. Who came? Dana. I can swim, and Mia can too.

73. Modifier attachment and ambiguity: English modifiers can attach to different words or phrases, changing interpretation. Examples: I saw the man with a telescope. The old men and women waited. Put the book on the table in the box.

74. Preposition polysemy: English prepositions express many meanings depending on context. Examples: The cup is on the table. The meeting is on Monday. She relied on her friend.

75. Focus particles: English uses words such as only, even, also, and too to highlight alternatives and restrict or expand meaning. Examples: Only Dana solved the puzzle. Even Leo passed the test. Maya also brought snacks.

76. Presupposition triggers: English can imply background assumptions through words such as again, stop, still, and definite descriptions. Examples: Nora stopped smoking. The king of the island is asleep. The baby is crying again.

77. Exceptions and exceptives: English states general claims with excluded cases. Examples: Every child except Sam laughed. All rooms but one are empty. No one besides Tara knew the code.

78. Reflexives and reciprocals: English expresses actions directed back to the same participant or mutually between participants. Examples: Ana washed herself. The twins helped each other. The guests introduced themselves.

79. Non-intersective and intensional adjectives: Some adjectives do not simply add an ordinary property to the noun. Examples: Nadia is a former mayor. This is a fake diamond. Leo is an alleged spy.

80. Vagueness and approximation: English allows imprecise boundaries, estimates, and approximate quantities. Examples: The meeting starts around noon. About twenty people came. The box is fairly heavy.

81. Scalar and gradable predicates: English uses adjectives and verbs that vary by degree and context. Examples: The coffee is warm. The mountain is high. The task is difficult.

82. Comparative correlatives and proportional change: English relates changes in one quantity to changes in another. Examples: The more you practice, the better you play. The faster the car goes, the louder the engine sounds.

83. Inclusion, exclusion, and set membership: English indicates whether entities are included in or excluded from groups. Examples: Maria is on the committee. The list includes three names. Ben is not among the finalists.
