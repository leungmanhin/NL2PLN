1. Entity classification: English states that individuals belong to categories, kinds, or classes. Examples: A robin is a bird. Maria is a doctor. This object is a vehicle.

2. Entity identification and naming: Proper names, titles, and labels identify particular individuals, places, or organizations. Examples: Alice arrived. Paris is in France. Dr. Chen called yesterday.

3. Properties and attributes: English assigns qualities, colors, sizes, ages, and other attributes to entities. Examples: The box is heavy. The apple is green. Sam is tall.

4. Relational properties: Some properties relate one entity to another rather than describing one entity alone. Examples: Anna is taller than Ben. The key is useful for the door. The road is parallel to the river.

5. Possession and ownership: English expresses ownership, custody, association, and belonging. Examples: Lena owns a bicycle. The teacher has a red pen. Maya's phone is on the table.

6. Part-whole relations: English describes components, membership, and physical or abstract parts of wholes. Examples: The car has four wheels. A chapter is part of the book. The handle belongs to the cup.

7. Identity and equivalence: English can state that two descriptions refer to the same entity or value. Examples: Clark Kent is Superman. The winner is Julia. This number equals ten.

8. Existence and nonexistence: English asserts that something exists, occurs, or is absent. Examples: There is a cat in the garden. A solution exists. There are no tickets left.

9. States and stative predication: English describes conditions or states that hold rather than events that unfold. Examples: The door is open. Nina knows French. The soup contains salt.

10. Actions and events: English describes happenings with participants such as agents and patients. Examples: The dog chased the cat. Omar opened the window. The storm damaged the roof.

11. Thematic roles: Event descriptions assign roles such as agent, patient, theme, recipient, source, goal, instrument, and beneficiary. Examples: Priya gave Leo a book. The chef cut bread with a knife. Sara sent a letter to Milan.

12. Transitive and intransitive events: Some verbs take direct objects, while others describe events without one. Examples: The baby slept. The player kicked the ball. The glass broke.

13. Ditransitive and transfer events: English expresses transfer of objects, information, or possession among participants. Examples: Emma gave Noah a ticket. The manager sent the team an email. Liam taught Ava chess.

14. Instrumental relations: English marks tools or means used to perform actions. Examples: He opened the can with a knife. They traveled by train. She wrote the note using a pencil.

15. Beneficiaries and maleficiaries: English identifies who benefits from or is harmed by an event. Examples: I baked a cake for Mia. The policy helped students. The mistake cost Daniel money.

16. Experiencer and stimulus roles: Perception, emotion, and cognition often involve an experiencer and a stimulus. Examples: Nina saw the comet. The noise frightened the child. Marco likes jazz.

17. Agentless and natural events: Some events occur without an explicit intentional agent. Examples: The ice melted. It rained all night. The vase fell from the shelf.

18. Passive voice: English can background the agent and foreground the affected participant. Examples: The window was broken by Tom. The cake was eaten. The report was approved yesterday.

19. Causative alternation: English often alternates between an entity changing state and another entity causing that change. Examples: The door opened. Julia opened the door. The water boiled.

20. Causation: English expresses that one event or state brings about another. Examples: The storm caused a delay. Pressing the switch started the machine. Lack of rain killed the crops.

21. Purpose and intention: English expresses goals, intended outcomes, and reasons for actions. Examples: Dana left early to catch the train. I bought flour for baking bread. They studied so that they could pass.

22. Result and consequence: English describes outcomes produced by actions or events. Examples: The glass fell and shattered. He trained hard, so he won. The road was icy, and several cars crashed.

23. Spatial containment: English expresses being inside, outside, or within bounded regions. Examples: The keys are in the drawer. The dog is inside the house. The coin is outside the box.

24. Spatial support and contact: English marks relations such as on, under, touching, and attached to. Examples: The book is on the table. The cat is under the chair. The poster is attached to the wall.

25. Spatial proximity and distance: English describes nearness, farness, adjacency, and separation. Examples: The bank is near the station. The school is far from the beach. The lamp is next to the sofa.

26. Spatial orientation and direction: English encodes left, right, above, below, in front of, behind, and facing. Examples: The picture is above the fireplace. The car is behind the truck. The statue faces the river.

27. Motion events: English describes movement with source, path, goal, and manner. Examples: The bird flew from the tree to the roof. Carla walked across the bridge. The ball rolled into the street.

28. Temporal location: English locates events at times, dates, and intervals. Examples: The meeting is at noon. Maya arrived on Monday. The store opens in July.

29. Temporal ordering: English expresses before, after, during, while, and simultaneous events. Examples: Ella washed the dishes before she slept. The alarm rang after midnight. Ben read while Anna cooked.

30. Duration and time span: English describes how long states or events last. Examples: The movie lasted two hours. He lived in Rome for a year. The child slept until morning.

31. Tense: English marks time of events relative to the speech time or another reference time. Examples: Nora walks to school. Nora walked to school. Nora will walk to school.

32. Aspect: English distinguishes ongoing, completed, habitual, and bounded event structure. Examples: Kim is reading a novel. Kim has read the novel. Kim used to read every night.

33. Frequency and habituality: English expresses how often events occur and whether they are customary. Examples: Raj jogs every morning. The bus usually arrives late. We visit them twice a year.

34. Existential quantification: English says that at least one entity satisfying a description exists. Examples: A student laughed. Someone called. There is a problem.

35. Universal quantification: English makes claims about all members of a relevant set. Examples: Every child received a gift. All birds need food. Each ticket has a number.

36. Cardinality and numerals: English specifies exact or approximate numbers of entities or events. Examples: Three cats slept on the sofa. I read two books. About twenty people came.

37. At-least and at-most quantification: English sets lower or upper numerical bounds. Examples: At least five students passed. No more than two guests may enter. Fewer than ten seats remain.

38. Proportional and vague quantifiers: English uses quantifiers such as most, many, few, several, and some. Examples: Most voters approved. Few people noticed. Several windows were open.

39. Partitives and measure expressions: English quantifies portions, amounts, and units of substances or groups. Examples: Half of the cake is gone. She drank two liters of water. A cup of rice is enough.

40. Distributive and collective readings: Plural descriptions can apply to individuals separately or to a group together. Examples: The students each wrote an essay. The students gathered in the hall. The boys carried the piano together.

41. Cumulative readings: Plural participants may be related across totals rather than one-to-one pairings. Examples: Three chefs cooked five dishes. The workers moved ten boxes. Two authors wrote six papers.

42. Generic statements: English makes generalizations about kinds or typical members rather than particular individuals. Examples: Tigers have stripes. A nurse helps patients. Dogs bark.

43. Definiteness and uniqueness: Articles and demonstratives signal familiarity, uniqueness, or identifiability. Examples: The moon is bright. I saw a dog; the dog was barking. This chair is broken.

44. Indefiniteness and specificity: Indefinite noun phrases may refer to a particular but unidentified entity or to any suitable entity. Examples: I am looking for a book. A student in my class won. Bring me a pencil.

45. Anaphora and pronoun reference: Pronouns and other referring expressions depend on earlier or contextual entities. Examples: Alice found a coin and kept it. Tom called Max because he was worried. The dog chased its tail.

46. Reflexives and reciprocals: English marks participants acting on themselves or on each other. Examples: Sara blamed herself. The boys helped each other. The mirrors reflected themselves.

47. Deixis: Some expressions depend on the speaker, addressee, time, or place of utterance. Examples: I am here now. Bring that book to me. This room is cold.

48. Negation: English denies propositions, events, properties, existence, or participation. Examples: The light is not on. Nobody came. Sam did not eat the cake.

49. Negative polarity and minimizers: Some expressions are licensed in negative or downward contexts. Examples: I did not see any birds. She has never lifted a finger to help. Few people ever complained.

50. Double negation and negative concord: Multiple negative expressions may cancel or reinforce depending on dialect and construction. Examples: It is not impossible. I did not say nothing. Nobody never called in that dialect.

51. Modality of ability: English expresses capacity, skill, or opportunity to act. Examples: Maya can swim. The door can open from inside. Leo is able to solve the puzzle.

52. Modality of possibility and probability: English expresses what may, might, could, or is likely to be true. Examples: It may rain tonight. The package might arrive early. The road is likely closed.

53. Modality of necessity and obligation: English expresses what must, has to, or needs to happen. Examples: You must wear a helmet. The form has to be signed. We need to leave now.

54. Permission and prohibition: English expresses what is allowed, forbidden, or authorized. Examples: You may enter. Students can use calculators. Visitors must not feed the animals.

55. Epistemic versus deontic modal readings: The same modal can express inference about truth or rules about action. Examples: She must be home by now. She must be home before ten. You can leave now.

56. Comparison of inequality: English compares entities or degrees using greater, lesser, more, less, and related forms. Examples: Ava is taller than Mia. This box is heavier than that one. Ben ran faster than Tom.

57. Equality and similarity comparison: English expresses equal degree, sameness, or resemblance. Examples: The two ropes are the same length. This copy is as clear as the original. Her voice sounds like mine.

58. Superlatives and extremes: English identifies maximal or minimal members along a scale. Examples: Omar is the tallest student. This is the cheapest ticket. Mount Everest is the highest mountain.

59. Ordinals and ranking: English orders entities by position in a sequence or hierarchy. Examples: Lina finished first. The second chapter is short. This is the third exit.

60. Degree modification: English modifies intensity, extent, or scalar values. Examples: The soup is very hot. The road is slightly wet. The task is extremely difficult.

61. Approximation and precision: English expresses exactness, approximation, and uncertainty about values. Examples: The box weighs exactly ten kilos. About fifty people attended. The trip took roughly an hour.

62. Conjunction: English combines entities, predicates, events, or clauses with and and similar expressions. Examples: Alice and Bob arrived. The flag is red and white. She cooked dinner and washed dishes.

63. Disjunction: English presents alternatives using or, either, and related forms. Examples: Tea or coffee is available. Either Sam or Lee will drive. You may call or email.

64. Exclusive and inclusive alternatives: Disjunction may allow one or both options, or exactly one option. Examples: You can have soup or salad. Choose either the red key or the blue key. The form may be signed by a parent or guardian.

65. Coordination scope: Coordinated expressions may share modifiers, subjects, objects, or operators in different ways. Examples: Old men and women waited outside. Kim bought and read a book. Alice likes tea and Bob coffee.

66. Conditionals: English expresses dependencies between antecedents and consequents. Examples: If it rains, the game will stop. You can enter if you have a ticket. If the light is red, cars must stop.

67. Counterfactual conditionals: English describes hypothetical situations contrary to fact or uncertain reality. Examples: If I had wings, I would fly. If she had left earlier, she would have arrived. If the vase were stronger, it would not break.

68. Unless and exceptive conditions: English marks negative or exceptional conditions. Examples: I will go unless it rains. Everyone passed except Tom. You cannot enter without a badge.

69. Propositional attitudes of belief and knowledge: English reports mental relations to propositions. Examples: Mia believes that the train is late. Noah knows that the door is locked. I suspect that he forgot.

70. Desires, wants, and preferences: English expresses goals, wishes, and preferred states of affairs. Examples: Ava wants a puppy. Luis hopes that it snows. I prefer tea to coffee.

71. Intentions, plans, and decisions: English describes commitments or planned future actions. Examples: Sara intends to study medicine. We plan to leave tomorrow. He decided to sell the car.

72. Speech and reporting events: English attributes utterances, questions, promises, requests, and claims to speakers. Examples: Dana said that she was tired. The guard asked whether we had tickets. Leo promised to help.

73. Factivity and presupposition in attitudes: Some verbs imply the truth of their complements, while others do not. Examples: Kim realized that the door was open. Pat regretted that he was late. Pat believed that he was late.

74. Questions: English asks for truth values, entities, quantities, times, reasons, or choices. Examples: Is the door open? Who ate the cake? Which train leaves first?

75. Imperatives and requests: English directs an addressee to perform an action or grants instructions. Examples: Close the window. Please sit down. Give me the file.

76. Adverbs of manner: English describes how an action is performed. Examples: The child walked slowly. She answered carefully. The engine runs smoothly.

77. Adverbs of degree, time, and place: Adverbs modify extent, timing, or location of events and states. Examples: He almost fell. They arrived yesterday. We searched everywhere.

78. Focus-sensitive expressions: Words such as only, even, also, and too depend on alternatives in context. Examples: Only Maria passed. Even Tom laughed. Ana also bought a ticket.

79. Restrictive and nonrestrictive modification: Modifiers may narrow reference or add extra information. Examples: Students who studied passed. My brother, who lives in Boston, called. The red car stopped.

80. Relative clauses: English uses clauses to modify nouns and identify entities. Examples: The woman who called is my aunt. I found the book that you recommended. The house where I grew up was sold.

81. Apposition: English places two noun phrases side by side to identify or describe the same entity. Examples: My friend Leo arrived. Paris, the capital of France, is crowded. The poet Sappho lived long ago.

82. Ellipsis and omitted material: English often leaves recoverable words or phrases unspoken. Examples: Kim ordered tea, and Lee coffee. I can swim, and Maya can too. Which book did you choose, the red one?

83. Gapping and sluicing: Some coordinated or interrogative structures omit verbal or clausal material. Examples: John likes apples, and Mary oranges. Someone called, but I do not know who. Ana went to Paris, and Ben to Rome.

84. Control constructions: Some verbs imply that an unspoken subject of an embedded action is controlled by another participant. Examples: Maya promised to leave. Leo persuaded Ana to leave. I tried to open the door.

85. Raising constructions: Some predicates make a participant of an embedded clause appear as the subject or object of the main clause. Examples: Sam seems to be tired. The plan appears to work. I believe Kim to be honest.

86. Event nominalization: English turns events or states into nouns that still imply participants and timing. Examples: The destruction of the bridge shocked us. Maria's arrival was late. The signing of the treaty ended the war.

87. Prepositional attachment ambiguity: Prepositional phrases can modify different words or phrases. Examples: I saw the man with a telescope. She put the book on the table. The report about the accident on Tuesday was confusing.

88. Lexical ambiguity and polysemy: Single words may have multiple related or unrelated meanings. Examples: The bank closed early. The fisherman sat on the bank. The mouse is under the desk.

89. Phrasal verbs and multiword predicates: English uses verb-particle or fixed multiword expressions with meanings not always predictable from parts. Examples: She looked up the word. The plane took off. He gave up.

90. Idioms and figurative fixed expressions: Some phrases have conventional meanings distinct from their literal words. Examples: Kim kicked the bucket. The plan is up in the air. Do not spill the beans.

91. Metaphor and metonymy: English often refers indirectly through associated concepts or figurative mappings. Examples: The White House issued a statement. Time is running out. The kettle is boiling.

92. Count and mass nouns: English distinguishes countable individuals from substances or masses. Examples: I bought three apples. I bought some rice. There is water on the floor.

93. Units, dimensions, and measurements: English expresses length, weight, temperature, speed, money, and other measured quantities. Examples: The rope is five meters long. The coffee costs three dollars. The car traveled sixty miles per hour.

94. Calendrical and named temporal expressions: English refers to structured calendar times and named occasions. Examples: The concert is on April fifth. We met last Christmas. The deadline is next Friday.

95. Aggregation and collective quantities: English expresses totals, averages, sums, and group-level measures. Examples: The team scored ten points. The average score was eighty. Together the bags weigh twenty kilos.

96. Exceptions and exclusions: English specifies members left out of a generalization or set. Examples: Everyone came except Nora. All rooms but one are clean. Apart from Tom, nobody objected.

97. Scalar particles and threshold expressions: English marks minimal, maximal, or surprising points on a scale. Examples: At least one person smiled. She has only three coins. Even a child can lift it.

98. Similarity, difference, and contrast: English compares entities by shared or contrasting properties. Examples: These shirts are different colors. The twins look alike. Unlike Ben, Ana enjoys chess.

99. Coreference across clauses: Multiple noun phrases may refer to the same entity across a sentence or discourse. Examples: Alice said that Alice was ready. Alice said that she was ready. The president met the senator before the president spoke.

100. Discourse relations: English connects sentences through explanation, contrast, elaboration, and sequence. Examples: The road was closed. Therefore, we turned back. It was raining, but we continued.

101. Context-dependent domain restriction: Quantifiers and descriptions often depend on an implicit relevant domain. Examples: Everyone is here. All the plates are dirty. No seats are available.

102. Polarity and truth-value operators beyond negation: English includes affirmation, denial, contradiction, and confirmation. Examples: Yes, the gate is open. It is true that she left. That claim is false.

103. Uncertainty and hedging: English marks weak commitment, approximation, or tentative claims. Examples: I think the key is upstairs. The meeting is probably canceled. It seems that she is tired.

104. Comparative subdeletion and differential comparison: English compares degrees while mentioning differences or missing measure phrases. Examples: The table is two inches wider than the door. More people came than chairs were available. She is taller than I expected.

105. Temporal recurrence and schedules: English describes repeated events tied to schedules or cycles. Examples: The train leaves every hour. Rent is due on the first of each month. The market opens on Saturdays.

106. Change of state: English describes transitions into or out of properties, locations, or conditions. Examples: The sky became dark. The milk turned sour. The door closed.

107. Creation and destruction events: English distinguishes bringing entities into existence and eliminating them. Examples: The artist painted a portrait. The factory built a bridge. The fire destroyed the barn.

108. Information structure and topic-comment organization: English signals what the sentence is about and what new information is asserted. Examples: As for the budget, it was approved. The book, I have not read. It was Maria who found the key.
